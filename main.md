\section{Related Work on Theorem Prover Testing}
\label{sec:bg_related}

Systematic testing of theorem provers and proof assistants has received less 
attention than testing of compilers and SMT solvers. The most directly 
relevant prior work spans three threads: SMT and compiler testing, 
hammer-style integration tools across different proof assistants, and 
benchmark-driven evaluation of automated theorem provers. Each thread 
informs a different design decision in the present project.

\paragraph{SMT and compiler testing.}
The most mature line of work is in the SMT domain. Brummayer and 
Biere~\cite{brummayer2009fuzzing} pioneered fuzzing and delta-debugging of 
SMT solvers, discovering numerous bugs in mainline implementations. Winterer, 
Zhang, and Su~\cite{winterer2020smt} subsequently introduced semantic fusion, 
a technique that combines two SMT formulas with known satisfiability into a 
single formula whose satisfiability is logically determined, allowing 
soundness violations to be detected without an external oracle. In the 
compiler domain, Yang et al.~\cite{yang2011csmith} developed CSmith, a 
grammar-aware random C program generator that discovered hundreds of bugs in 
production compilers through differential testing. These three works 
collectively establish that grammar-aware generation, differential testing, 
and semantics-preserving transformations are reliable techniques for finding 
defects in mature reasoning tools. Their direct influence on this project is 
visible in the differential testing harness of Section~\ref{sec:design_differential} 
and in the grammar-based seed generation used in the SMT phase 
(Section~\ref{sec:design_smtfuzz}). However, the negative result of the SMT 
phase --- approximately 25{,}000 rounds of grammar-aware mutation yielded no 
novel soundness defects --- exposed a limitation that the prior literature 
does not emphasise: when the target's input grammar is sufficiently rigid 
\emph{and} the population of seed inputs is small, the mutation space 
collapses onto a region the developers have already covered with their own 
regression tests. This observation motivates the source-informed pivot 
described in Chapter~\ref{ch:implementation}, and is, to the author's 
knowledge, not articulated as such in the SMT-fuzzing literature.

\paragraph{Benchmark-driven testing of ATPs.}
For ATPs specifically, the testing landscape is dominated by benchmark-driven 
evaluation rather than fuzzing. The TPTP benchmark library~\cite{sutcliffe2009tptp} 
and the CADE ATP System Competition (CASC) provide a form of ongoing 
regression testing for individual provers, but they exercise each prover 
with curated academic problems rather than the integration-specific inputs 
generated by tools such as Sledgehammer. The coverage analysis in 
Section~\ref{sec:eval_cov_eprover} quantifies this gap precisely for E~prover: 
standard TPTP benchmarks exercise 33.0\% of the lines in 
\texttt{ccl\_clauses.c}, while Sledgehammer-generated inputs reach only 0.4\% 
--- yet it is the latter, narrow slice that contains the bug fixed by 
commit \texttt{ebb74302}. The implication is that conventional benchmark 
testing and integration-layer testing are \emph{complementary} rather than 
overlapping: a prover can rank highly at CASC and still ship with 
integration-path defects that affect every Sledgehammer user. This 
quantitative reframing of benchmark adequacy is one of the project's 
methodological contributions.

\paragraph{Hammer-style tools across proof assistants.}
A complementary perspective comes from the broader \emph{hammer} ecosystem. 
Hammer-style tools share a common architecture --- premise selection, 
translation to an external prover, and proof reconstruction --- and have 
been developed for several proof assistants beyond Isabelle. Kaliszyk and 
Urban~\cite{kaliszyk2015mizar40} described MizAR for Mizar, demonstrating 
that machine-learned premise selection paired with E and Vampire could prove 
a substantial fraction of the Mizar Mathematical Library automatically. 
Czajka and Kaliszyk~\cite{czajka2018coqhammer} brought a similar architecture 
to Coq with CoqHammer, contending with the additional challenge of 
translating dependent type theory into first-order logic. Carneiro, Brown, 
and Urban~\cite{carneiro2023metamath} extended the approach to Metamath, and 
Zhu et al.~\cite{zhu2025leanhammer} most recently introduced LeanHammer, the 
first end-to-end domain-general hammer for Lean --- notably reusing 
Zipperposition as the underlying ATP. The recurrence of Zipperposition 
across multiple hammer ecosystems amplifies the practical impact of 
defects discovered in this project: a parser or unification bug in 
Zipperposition is no longer an Isabelle-only concern, but propagates to 
every downstream hammer that adopts it as a backend. None of these hammer 
papers, however, reports systematic interface-level testing of the 
prover--host translation layer; they evaluate hammers by proof rate on 
benchmark libraries, which measures success but not the structural 
robustness of the integration. The methodology developed in this project 
therefore generalises to those settings as well, and is identified in 
Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion} as a direction for future work.

\paragraph{Isabelle's own integration testing.}
Within Isabelle itself, the development team conducts integration testing 
via \texttt{isabelle build} across a large corpus of formalisations, which 
exercises Sledgehammer indirectly. However, this testing treats Sledgehammer 
as a black box: a session passes if its lemmas are proved and fails if they 
are not, with no introspection into which prover succeeded, why, or whether 
the prover's response was structurally well-formed. Three of the four 
findings reported in this project (Bugs~\#1, \#3, and \#4) would survive 
this style of testing indefinitely, because the affected code paths 
either never reach kernel reconstruction (parser failures) or are masked 
by parallel prover invocations (where a successful slice from one prover 
hides a failed slice from another). The source-informed methodology adopted 
here therefore complements rather than replaces existing practice, and the 
quantitative comparison in Section~\ref{sec:eval_methods} is intended to 
characterise the regions of the defect space each approach is suited to.
